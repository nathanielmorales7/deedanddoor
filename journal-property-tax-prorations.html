DFW property tax bills are out. Here's how prorations work, and how to protest your value.

Oct 18, 2026 · 5 min read
Photo by Karola G · Pexels

When do DFW property tax bills get mailed, and when are they due?

Property tax bills across Dallas, Tarrant, Collin, and Denton counties go out in October every year. Payment is due by January 31 of the following year, and penalties start at 6% of the balance owed on February 1, climbing the longer a bill sits unpaid, according to Community Impact's reporting on the statewide deadline.

Homeowners with an escrow account built into their mortgage usually never see the bill at all — the mortgage company receives it directly and pays it out of that account, while the homeowner gets a courtesy copy at most. The Dallas County Tax Office confirms this is standard practice for escrowed accounts. Denton County's tax office adds one more wrinkle worth knowing: a mailed payment has to be postmarked by January 31, not just received by then, to count as on time.

What happens to the tax bill when a home changes hands partway through the year?

Texas taxes a home for the full calendar year, no matter who owns it on any given day inside that year. When a home sells in, say, June, neither the buyer nor the seller owes the county "half" a bill directly — the full-year bill still goes out to whoever owns the home when it's mailed in October. What the buyer and seller actually divide between themselves, at closing, is the cost of that bill. That split is called a proration, and both the standard TREC and TAR contracts already spell out how it works before either side signs.

How is the tax proration actually calculated at closing?

Which number gets used depends on timing. For closings before April 1, the title company uses last year's tax amount, since that's the most recent real figure available. For closings after April 1 but before the new bill is printed, it multiplies the county appraisal district's new market value by last year's tax rate. Once the actual current-year bill exists, that figure replaces the estimate — per Independence Title's rundown of how Texas title companies handle this.

Because most DFW closings happen well before the October bill comes out, the seller almost always ends up giving the buyer a credit at closing — covering the seller's share of the year, from January 1 through the closing date — rather than paying anything to the county that day. New construction and homes that just lost an exemption, such as a seller who no longer qualified for the over-65 exemption, don't have a clean prior-year number to pull from, so those get calculated by hand instead of pulled straight from the appraisal district's system.

Who actually pays the property tax bill after closing?

The buyer is the one who pays the bill once it's mailed in October, not the seller, even though the seller already handed over a credit for part of the year at closing. If the buyer's loan includes an escrow account, the lender collects a portion of the estimated tax bill with every mortgage payment and pays the county directly when the bill arrives — the seller's closing credit simply becomes part of what funds that account early on.

How much does the homestead exemption save on this year's bill?

A primary residence in Texas gets $140,000 knocked off its value before school district taxes are calculated, and the school district is usually the single largest line item on a DFW tax bill. Homeowners who are 65 or older, or who have a qualifying disability, get an additional $60,000 on top of that — $200,000 off their home's taxable value for school purposes. The $140,000 figure is new: voters raised it from $100,000 through a constitutional amendment in Proposition 13, passed in November 2025, and the increase applies retroactively back to January 1, 2025.

What's pushing some DFW tax bills higher this year, even where the headline rate barely moved?

A single property tax bill is really the sum of several separate taxing entities — the county, the city, the school district, and often a community college or hospital district too — and each one sets its own rate independently. Reporting from the Dallas Express shows how differently those pieces moved for 2026: Tarrant County College raised its rate 6.88%, while Tarrant County's own rate barely changed. Dallas County raised its rate by about 15.4%, enough to trigger a November 3 voter-approval election, while Dallas College held its own rate exactly flat.

Two homes with similar values in different parts of DFW can see noticeably different year-over-year changes on their bill for exactly this reason — the county's own rate is only one piece of what's stacked on top of a property.

How can a homeowner protest their appraised value to lower next year's bill?

The deadline to protest is May 15, or 30 days after the county mails the Notice of Appraised Value, whichever date is later — and those notices typically go out in April, well before the October tax bill itself. Filing happens directly with the county's own appraisal district: Dallas Central Appraisal District, Tarrant Appraisal District, Collin Central Appraisal District, or Denton Central Appraisal District, depending on where the home sits.

The strongest evidence is usually recent sales of comparable nearby homes from the past six to twelve months, especially ones that sold for less than the county's appraised value. Photos documenting real condition issues — foundation problems, an outdated kitchen, roof damage — and a recent appraisal from a refinance can help too, per this breakdown of the protest process. Most counties start with an informal review before anything escalates to a formal Appraisal Review Board hearing, and filing doesn't require hiring anyone — a homeowner can make the case directly.
