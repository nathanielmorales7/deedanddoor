My listing Expired, what now?

My Home Didn't Sell

Your listing expired, and that stings — you cleaned for months, disappeared every weekend for showings, and the sign came down without a sale. Before you decide anything about what's next, it's worth understanding exactly why it happened. Almost every expired listing traces back to one of four things, and none of them mean something is wrong with your home.

The autopsy

Why didn't my home sell?

This is the honest answer more often than any seller wants to hear, and it's rarely about greed — it's usually about anchoring to what the house "should" be worth, or to what a neighbor's house sold for two years ago in a different rate environment. Buyers today compare your home to what's active and pending right now, not to what things cost when you bought.

A telltale sign: lots of online views and saves, but few or no showings. That pattern almost always means the price is the first thing scaring buyers off before they ever walk through the door.

The opposite pattern — plenty of showings, but no offers, or an offer or two that quietly disappeared — usually points somewhere else: condition, presentation, or how the listing was marketed. If the online photos didn't do the home justice, buyers may have skipped it before ever scheduling a tour, and the ones who did show up may have been comparing it unfavorably to better-staged competition.

Worth an honest gut-check: were there professional photos? A floor plan? A description that actually highlighted what makes the home worth touring, not just a list of square footage and appliances?

A home that's hard to show is a home that doesn't sell — it's that simple. Restricted showing windows, requiring 24-hour notice, or a pet that made agents nervous to bring buyers through can quietly kill a listing without anyone realizing that's what happened. Buyers' agents are juggling multiple showings in an afternoon; a listing that's inconvenient to access just gets skipped in favor of one that isn't.

Sometimes it really is the calendar's fault. Listings that launch right before a holiday, during a rate spike, or into a sudden wave of new competing inventory in the same subdivision face a smaller buyer pool through no fault of the price or the home itself. This one's frustrating because there's nothing to "fix" — just a better window to try again in.

Nothing dramatic — the listing agreement simply ends on the date you both agreed to, and the home comes off active MLS status. You're not obligated to relist with the same agent, and you're free to sit out the market entirely for a while if that's what you'd rather do. The one thing worth double-checking is your old listing agreement's protection period language — most include a short window (often 30-90 days) where a commission could still be owed if you sell to a buyer that agent specifically introduced during the listing.

Yes, you can relist immediately — there's no waiting period. Whether to reuse the same agent is a real question worth sitting with honestly. If the marketing and communication were strong and it really was a pricing or timing issue, sticking with them and adjusting strategy can make sense. If you were left wondering what was actually being done on your behalf, a fresh set of eyes (and a fresh MLS listing history) is a completely reasonable call.

Buyers' agents can see a property's listing history, including a prior expiration, and a small number will ask about it. That's manageable — a fresh listing with new photos, a corrected price, and an honest answer ready ("we adjusted the price and marketing based on feedback") reads as a seller who learned something, not a red flag. What actually damages a relist is repeating the exact same strategy the first time around and hoping for a different result.

If I were in the seller's shoes going into a relist, here's what I'd want done: pull current comparables instead of the ones from the original listing, get new professional photos even if the old ones seemed fine, and ask directly for the showing feedback the previous agent collected — it's usually more honest and more useful than anything a seller hears secondhand. Small, specific fixes based on real feedback beat a blanket price cut almost every time.
