Three pre-listing repairs that pay for themselves — and three that don't

Oct 10, 2026 · 4 min read
Photo by JESHOOTS.com · Pexels

Not every pre-sale project is worth the cost. The 2025 Cost vs. Value Report — compiled by Remodeling magazine and Zonda Home, tracking 28 projects against actual resale value across 119 U.S. markets — found a wide gap between the best and worst performers. Some repairs return more than sellers spent on them. Others return less than half.

Which repairs actually pay for themselves before selling?

Three projects stood out nationally: a new garage door recouped about 268% of its cost at resale, a steel entry door recouped about 216%, and manufactured stone veneer on the front exterior recouped about 208%. All three are small, visible curb-appeal fixes, not full renovations.

ProjectAverage costAdded at resaleCost recouped
Garage door replacement$4,672$12,507268%
Steel entry door replacement$2,435$5,270216%
Manufactured stone veneer$11,702$24,328208%

These are national averages, not DFW-specific figures. The underlying pattern holds here too, and Florida Realtors covered the same report this summer with the same takeaway: buyers form an opinion about a house before they're through the front door, and a tired garage door shapes that opinion more than its price tag suggests.

Which renovations lose sellers the most money?

On the other end, an upscale primary suite addition recouped only about 18% of its cost, a midrange version of the same project recouped about 32%, and an upscale major kitchen remodel recouped about 36%. Large additions and luxury-grade remodels consistently returned less than half of what they cost.

ProjectAverage costAdded at resaleCost recouped
Primary suite addition (upscale)$351,613$63,13618%
Primary suite addition (midrange)$170,517$55,09732%
Major kitchen remodel (upscale)$164,104$58,56136%

Why do small fixes outperform big remodels?

Cost recouped shrinks as a project's price tag grows, because buyers are pricing a home against its condition and its competition on the market, not reimbursing a seller for whatever they personally spent on it. A new garage door is easy for any buyer to value at a glance. A $350,000 primary suite addition is a different story — it often reflects one owner's personal taste and timeline more than what the next buyer is willing to pay for.

Is a kitchen or bathroom update ever worth it before listing?

Sometimes. A minor kitchen remodel — new cabinet fronts, countertops, and appliances, without moving plumbing or walls — recouped about 113% of its cost, the only kitchen or bathroom project in the report that returned more than it cost. A full gut renovation didn't come close, even at the midrange tier, which recouped about 51%.

Scope decides the outcome more than the room does. Fixing cracked floor tile, patching dinged-up paint, or swapping out dated cabinet hardware — updating what's already there — outperforms tearing it out and starting over almost every time a seller is weighing the two against each other.

What should sellers do instead of a big renovation?

Put the money into what shows up in every listing photo instead: a deep clean, fresh paint in neutral tones, fixed trim and touch-up repairs, and decluttered rooms. Those moves cost far less than a remodel and tend to matter more to how buyers perceive the home than any single big-ticket project.
