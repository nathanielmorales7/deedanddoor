Three showings, three lessons buyers keep learning the hard way

Aug 8, 2026 · 4 min read

Every buyer's tour eventually runs into the same handful of moments, no matter the price point or the neighborhood. Three of them come up more than any others — and each one is an easy trap to fall into if nobody points it out ahead of time.

Why do buyers keep falling for a home's finishes over its bones?

Fresh paint, new fixtures, and staged furniture are the fastest way to make a buyer fall for a home in the first 90 seconds — and the fastest way to distract from a foundation crack, an aging roof, or a water stain tucked in a closet. Cosmetic updates are cheap to fix after closing; structural and mechanical problems are the expensive kind, which is exactly backwards from how most buyers weigh them on a first walkthrough.

The fix is simple but easy to forget in the moment: walk the home once for feel, then walk it a second time specifically looking for the boring stuff — foundation, roof age, HVAC age, water heater, any sign of past water damage.

What's the most common mistake buyers make when comparing showings?

Judging each home in isolation instead of against the others on the list is the single biggest reason buyers second-guess themselves after an offer. A kitchen that felt amazing in showing three can start to feel ordinary once you remember showing one actually had more storage — but only if you took real notes, because after four or five homes in a day, the details blur together fast.

Photos and a simple checklist beat memory every time. Score each home the same way, on the same categories, right after you walk out.

How should you actually use a showing to test a home, not just admire it?

Do the things you'll actually do once you live there: open every closet and cabinet, run the water long enough to check pressure and temperature, stand in the primary bedroom with the door closed to gauge real street noise, and check cell signal in the rooms you'll actually use. A 20-minute showing rarely surfaces these on its own — they take a few extra minutes of deliberately testing the space instead of just walking through it.

What questions should you ask on every showing but usually don't?

A short list worth asking on every single showing, not just the ones you're seriously considering:

  1. How old is the roof, and has it ever had storm damage or a claim filed?
  2. How old is the HVAC system, and has it been serviced regularly?
  3. Has the home ever had foundation work, and is there a transferable warranty?
  4. Why is the seller actually moving?
  5. How long has this specific listing been on the market, and has the price already dropped?

Not every listing agent will have every answer on the spot, but asking puts the question on record early — before it turns into a surprise during the option period.

How many showings does it typically take before making an offer?

There's no fixed number, but most buyers land somewhere between eight and fifteen showings before writing their first offer, with that count usually dropping fast once they've seen enough homes to actually know what they're comparing against. The first three or four showings often do more to calibrate expectations than to produce a serious contender — that's normal, not a sign anything's wrong with the search.

What's the one lesson that saves buyers the most money?

Get the inspection during the option period even on a home that looks perfect. The homes that cost buyers the most after closing are almost always the ones that looked flawless on the showing and never got a proper inspection, because everyone — buyer included — was too charmed to slow down and check.
