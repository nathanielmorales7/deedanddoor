Three showings, three lessons buyers keep learning the hard way

Aug 8, 2026 · 4 min read

After enough showings, certain patterns become impossible not to notice — not because any one buyer does something unusual, but because almost everyone runs into the same few traps on the way to an offer. None of these are exotic mistakes. They're the ordinary, easy-to-make ones, and they show up on a lot of showings, not just a few. Here are the three that come up most.

Why does a home feel different on the second walkthrough than the first?

The first walkthrough of a home you like is rarely an objective one — good staging, nice lighting, and a strong first impression are designed to make you feel something before you've actually evaluated anything. That's not a criticism of staging; it works because it's supposed to. The lesson is just to notice when it's happening: if you catch yourself mentally arranging furniture before you've checked the water pressure, opened every closet, or figured out where the washer and dryer actually go, it's worth slowing down. A second look — ideally without the agent who's selling it walking you through — almost always surfaces something the first visit didn't: a floor plan that doesn't flow the way you remembered, a bedroom that's smaller than it felt, storage that looked fine in photos and isn't.

Why does it matter what time of day you see a home?

A showing at 10am on a quiet Tuesday and the same house at 6pm on a Friday can feel like two different properties. Street parking that looked wide open might be gone by evening. A backyard that felt private at noon might back up to a school with pickup-line traffic twice a day. A bedroom that seemed dim and cool during an afternoon showing might bake in direct west-facing sun by early evening. If you're seriously considering an offer, a second visit at a different time of day — or even just driving by on your own before you're locked into a schedule — tells you things the official showing never will.

Do you really need to waive the inspection to compete right now?

A few years ago, waiving the inspection contingency felt almost mandatory to be competitive in DFW. That pressure has eased. According to NAR's own Realtors Confidence Index, just 16% of buyers nationally waived their inspection contingency in July 2026 — down from 20% the month before and 23% a year earlier. Most buyers, in other words, are still getting one, and that's the right instinct: an inspection is one of the few tools that gives you an objective, third-party read on a home instead of relying on how it felt walking through. In a market with more inventory and less pressure to write a same-day offer, there's rarely a good reason to skip it just because it once felt necessary to win.
