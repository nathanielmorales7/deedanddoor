What Texas Law Actually Requires When You Return a Tenant's Security Deposit

Oct 31, 2026 · 5 min read
Photo by Athena Sandrini · Pexels

This is general information, not legal advice. If you're dealing with an actual security deposit dispute, talk to a Texas landlord-tenant attorney about your specific situation.

Texas gives landlords 30 days to get a tenant's security deposit right. Miss that window, or skip a required step, and the law tips hard in the tenant's favor — including penalties that can cost far more than the deposit itself. So what's required?

How long does a landlord have to return a security deposit in Texas?

Thirty days. After a tenant moves out, a landlord must either refund the deposit or send a written, itemized list of deductions within that window (Texas Property Code § 92.103). That clock is the single most important number - nearly every penalty in the law traces back to missing it.

What if the tenant never gives a forwarding address?

The 30-day clock doesn't start without one. A landlord isn't required to refund the deposit or itemize charges until the tenant sends a written forwarding address (§ 92.107). That said, the tenant never loses the right to a refund or accounting just because they forgot to send it — it's worth following up, or sending the accounting to the rental address anyway if one never arrives.

What has to be in the itemized deduction list?

Every charge, in writing. If any part of the deposit is kept, the landlord must send an itemized list of each deduction (§ 92.104). The one exception: if the tenant owes unpaid rent at move-out and doesn't dispute the amount, the landlord can simply apply the deposit to that balance without a written itemization — there's nothing to itemize when the amount owed is already agreed on. Separately, landlords are required to keep accurate records of deposits received and how they were used (§ 92.106). Timestamped move-in and move-out photos aren't required by that section, but they're the best way to back up a deduction if it's ever disputed.

What can actually be deducted from a security deposit?

Unpaid rent and real damage — nothing else. Normal wear and tear can never be deducted (§ 92.104(b)): small nail holes, minor scuffs, worn carpet from foot traffic, and sun fading don't qualify. Larger holes, stains, burns, unauthorized paint, or negligence-caused damage do. The landlord carries the burden of proving a deduction was reasonable, not the tenant.

What happens if a landlord misses the deadline or skips the itemized list?

It can get expensive really fast. Missing the 30-day deadline creates a legal presumption of bad faith (§ 92.109(d)) — the landlord then has to prove otherwise. A landlord found to have acted in bad faith owes the tenant $100, three times whatever was wrongfully withheld, and the tenant's attorney's fees (§ 92.109(a)). Skip the itemized list in bad faith, and the penalty is simpler: the landlord forfeits the right to keep any of the deposit at all (§ 92.109(b)).

What are the most common mistakes that land DFW landlords in trouble?

A few mistakes repeat often. Sending the accounting late because there was no system tracking the 30-day clock. Deducting for normal wear and tear instead of real damage. Assuming a tenant can skip paying rent during the lease by claiming the deposit already covers it — the law doesn't allow that (§ 92.108). A tenant who gives notice and says "just keep my deposit instead of this month's rent" still owes that rent; the deposit isn't rent money until the tenancy actually ends. And having no move-in or move-out documentation when a deduction gets disputed.

How can a landlord protect themselves?

Don't wait for move-out day. The legal 30-day clock starts when the tenant surrenders the unit, but it's worth getting ready as soon as they give notice — schedule the move-out inspection, pull the move-in photos, and have the itemization process ready so there's no scramble once that clock actually starts. Take dated photos at move-in and move-out. Send the accounting by certified mail. And when a dispute is genuinely close, it's usually cheaper to settle it for a signed release than to find out what "three times the withheld amount plus attorney's fees" actually costs in a justice court.
